## 1. Three Concepts First: Withdrawal, Internal Transfer, Selling for Fiat

These three get mixed up constantly. They are very different:

| Action | Where the money goes | Needs an address? | On-chain fee? | Speed |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Internal transfer** | Another user's account on the same platform | No network choice; uses platform identifiers | Usually none | Usually fast |
| **On-chain withdrawal** | A wallet or exchange outside the platform | Yes, an on-chain address | Yes, depends on network conditions | Depends on congestion |
| **Selling for fiat** | Your bank account or payment channel | Uses payout details | Trading fee applies (at the sell) | Depends on the channel |

**Key point:**&#8203; a withdrawal moves assets **off the platform**. If you simply want to sell and get money, that is "sell + fiat payout" — an entirely different flow.

## 3. Two Kinds of Addresses: Internal and On-Chain

| Type | What it looks like | What it is for |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **On-chain address** | A long string of letters and digits (format varies by chain) | Sending to a self-custody wallet or another platform |
| **Internal address** | Usually a short ID, phone number, email, or account identifier | Sending to another user on the same platform |

> ⚠️ These **cannot be used interchangeably.** Pasting an on-chain address into an internal transfer field, or treating an internal ID as an on-chain address, will fail. Internal transfers are also generally irreversible.

## 4. What Is a Memo / Tag, and When Is It Required?

Some chains and assets ask for a **Memo (note) or Tag** in addition to the address.

**The rule is simple:**&#8203; if the recipient's page asks for a Memo, you must fill it in; if it does not ask, do not add one.

| Situation | What to do |
|:---|:---|
| The recipient (e.g. another exchange's deposit page) explicitly requires a Memo / Tag | **You must fill it in**, character for character |
| Withdrawing to a self-custody wallet | Usually not needed — leave it blank |
| You are not sure | **Ask first**, or send a tiny test amount |

Omitting a required Memo often results in "the chain says success, but the recipient never credited you" — and fixing that means contacting their support, which is a real hassle.

## 5. The Seven-Step Binance Withdrawal

### Step 1: Open the withdrawal page

Log in to the app or web, go to **Assets → Withdraw**, and select the asset you want to send (for example BTC or USDT).

### Step 2: Choose the withdrawal type

The interface usually lets you pick **on-chain withdrawal** or **internal transfer**. For your first time, pick the route you actually need and do not switch midway.

### Step 3: Enter the receiving address

**Copy and paste — never type it by hand.** After pasting, compare the first and last few characters. If the recipient provides a QR code, scanning is safer than manual entry.

### Step 4: Select the network (chain)

This is the critical step. The chain you select must match the recipient:

- Sending to another exchange → their deposit page states which chains it supports
- Sending to a self-custody wallet → the wallet shows which chain it uses

### Step 5: Enter the amount

The page displays the **minimum withdrawal, estimated fee, and the amount that will actually arrive**. For a first withdrawal, use a round number rather than "max" so it is easier to verify.

### Step 6: Complete security verification

This may include your password, two-factor authentication (2FA), and an email or SMS code. If you enabled an address whitelist, newly added addresses typically face a cooling-off period.

### Step 7: Confirm and track the confirmations

Check the withdrawal history for the status:

| Status | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| Pending review | The platform is processing it; risk checks or a new address can extend this |
| Processing | Sent to the network |
| Completed | On-chain; you can look it up in a block explorer with the transaction ID |

If it says completed but the recipient sees nothing, **verify the chain and address first**, then contact the receiving platform.

## 6. Three Rules for Choosing a Network

The same asset is often available on several networks. You may see names like **TRC20, ERC20, or BEP20** in the dropdown (actual availability **follows what the withdrawal page shows**).

| Rule | Explanation |
|:---|:---|
| **1. Compatibility first** | Use whatever chain the recipient supports. There are no exceptions here |
| **2. Cost second** | Network fees vary widely between chains — compare them only after compatibility is settled |
| **3. Speed last** | Congestion slows confirmations, but picking an incompatible chain for speed is the classic mistake |

**Remember: the chain is not about what suits you — it is about what the recipient can receive.**
