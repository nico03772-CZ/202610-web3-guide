## 1. What Position Sizing Actually Solves

Every trade decision has two layers:

- **Direction layer**: long or short, at what entry price — this decides whether you *can* profit;
- **Size layer**: how much capital, where the stop goes, what the maximum loss is — this decides whether you *survive long enough* to profit.

Beginners pour all their attention into direction and leave sizing to mood: heavy when confident, light when unsure. The same read then produces wildly different outcomes. Position sizing exists to turn "how much do I put on" from an emotional question into a calculated one.

## 2. Three Concepts to Separate First: Margin, Notional, Leverage

| Concept | Meaning | Why it matters |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Margin | Capital actually committed and tied up | Determines how much of your account the position occupies |
| Notional | Total contract value of the position | **Fees, point value, and P&L are all calculated on notional** |
| Leverage | Notional ÷ margin | Affects capital efficiency, not risk directly |

The key line: **risk comes from notional size and stop distance, not from the leverage multiple.**

For example, 1,000 USDT of margin at 10x equals 10,000 USDT notional; the same 1,000 USDT at 3x equals 3,000 USDT notional. A 10% adverse move hurts far more in the first case. So "I only use low leverage" is not the same as "my risk is small" — real risk depends on how big the notional is and how wide the stop is.

## 3. The Five-Step Framework

### Step 1: Set a Risk Cap Per Trade

Before sizing anything, fix one number: **the maximum percentage of your account a single trade may lose.**

- A common approach is to keep risk per trade in the 1%–2% range (illustrative range, adjust to your own tolerance);
- Once set, this number must not float with conviction — scaling up your risk budget when you feel confident is the single most common path to liquidation;
- Critical precondition: **without a stop loss you cannot size a position.** The stop comes first; the size follows.

### Step 2: Derive Size From Stop Distance

The simplified formulas:

```
Affordable loss = account equity × risk per trade %
Notional size   = affordable loss ÷ stop distance %
```

And in isolated margin mode:

```
Required margin ≈ notional size ÷ leverage
```

The order matters: decide how much you may lose, decide how wide the stop is, then derive the size. That's the exact reverse of "pick a size first and hope the loss is acceptable."

### Step 3: Control Total Exposure and Correlation

You can size a single trade perfectly and still lose control of the account by holding too many positions in the same direction:

- **Correlated instruments move together**: BTC and major coins often rise and fall together in violent moves. Five positions in the same direction aren't diversification — they're one position multiplied by five;
- **Set a total exposure cap**: define the maximum loss if *every* open position hits its stop simultaneously, and keep it within a tolerable share of your account;
- **Run a correlation check**: before opening, ask whether the new trade is really the same bet as what you already hold.

### Step 4: Scaling In and Add/Reduce Rules

- **Scale in**: split the planned size into two or three entries at key levels to reduce the impact of a single entry price;
- **Add only on profit**: add only when the position is in profit *and* the stop can be moved up accordingly. After adding, total risk must stay within the original per-trade cap;
- **Averaging down a loss**: the most common way to lose control. It keeps enlarging notional and dilutes your stop, so when the trend continues against you, losses compound far faster than when you entered;
- **Reducing is risk control**: when uncertain, cutting size is the most direct risk control available — more effective than fiddling with leverage.

### Step 5: Journal and Review

The final step is keeping the numbers:

| Record | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| Entry, stop, target | Check the stop distance matched the plan |
| Notional size, margin used | Check per-trade and total exposure caps |
| Worst-case loss for the trade | Check it stayed within the risk budget |
| Actual outcome and deviation | Find where planned size and actual size diverged |

The point of review is spotting patterns: most people's large losses come not from one bad call, but from repeatedly oversized positions.

## 6. Five Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Treating margin as risk | Assuming 100 USDT of margin means the most you can lose is 100 USDT | P&L is calculated on notional, so losses can far exceed the margin |
| Confusing leverage with size | Believing "low leverage = safe" or "high leverage = big position" | Ignores notional size and stop distance, which actually determine risk |
| Calling same-direction positions diversified | Holding longs in several highly correlated coins | Correlation stacks your risk into one large position |
| Averaging down a loss | Adding to a losing position to lower the average | Notional balloons and the stop is diluted |
| Sizing without a stop | Entering first and thinking about the stop later | Size can't be derived, risk can't be controlled |

**Q3: Isn't a 1% risk cap too conservative to make money?**&#8203;
A risk cap is a survival metric, not a return metric. It determines how many consecutive losses you can absorb. Returns come from your strategy's expectancy and your discipline, not from the size of any single bet.

**Q4: How should I set my stop distance?**&#8203;
Typically from market structure (prior lows, key support) and the instrument's current volatility — not by deciding how much you're willing to lose and forcing a stop to fit. Once the stop is set, the size follows.

**Q5: Is holding several coins at once diversification?**&#8203;
Only if correlation is low. Major crypto assets often move together in violent markets, so multiple same-direction positions stack into one large position.

**Q6: Can I average down when I'm losing?**&#8203;
It's not advisable. Averaging down enlarges notional and dilutes your stop — the most common route to losing control. If you want less risk, reducing is more direct than adding.

**Q7: When can I add to a position?**&#8203;
When it's in profit and you can move the stop up to a controlled level. After adding, total risk should not exceed your original per-trade cap.

**Q8: Does the 58% fee discount change position sizing?**&#8203;
It lowers the cost per trade but doesn't change the risk math. Size should still be determined by your risk budget and stop distance — never enlarged just because costs dropped.

**Q9: Does scaling in raise my fees?**&#8203;
Yes. Scaling in means more trades, so total fees usually rise. For small positions subject to minimum order limits, a single entry is often more practical.

**Q10: Does position sizing guarantee I won't get liquidated?**&#8203;
No. It significantly reduces the size of any single loss and extends your account's survival time, but it can't change market direction or remove liquidation risk. Gaps and slippage in extreme conditions can still cause losses larger than expected.
